The Old Masters'
Portrait Notes

Five lessons I give at the start of every portrait workshop. They work with a pencil and printer paper, and they work with charcoal on toned paper. Read one, then draw for twenty minutes.

What you need

A soft pencil (2B–4B) or vine charcoal
Toned or plain paper
A white pastel or white charcoal pencil
A kneaded eraser

I.Measure before you draw

The Old Masters didn't guess. Hold your pencil at arm's length and compare: how many eye-widths fit across the face? Where does the bottom of the nose sit between the brows and the chin? Most faces are close to the classical thirds, hairline to brows, brows to nose, nose to chin, but the face in front of you is never exactly average. The likeness lives in the small differences.

Try thisDraw only an oval and three horizontal lines for the brows, nose and mouth. Check each line against your reference twice before you draw a single feature.

II.See the planes, not the features

A face is a set of surfaces turning toward and away from the light: the front of the forehead, the side of the nose, the cheek plane under the eye. Beginners draw an outline of an eye. Painters see the eye socket as a shadow shape first, with the eye sitting inside it.

Try thisSquint at your reference until details disappear. Draw the three or four biggest shadow shapes as flat patches before any detail.

III.Keep values in families

Decide early where the light ends. Everything in the light stays light, everything in the shadow stays dark, even the "bright" parts of the shadow side. When a portrait looks flat, it's usually because a light and a dark were mixed across that border.

Try thisMake a three-step scale: the paper, a middle grey and your darkest dark. Shade a whole portrait using only those three.

IV.Lose some edges

Not every line deserves to be sharp. Keep the hardest edges where the eye should land, usually around the eyes and the corner of the mouth, and let the edges soften where the form turns gently, like the cheek melting into shadow. Soft edges are what make a drawing breathe.

Try thisWith your finger or a tissue, soften every edge in the drawing except three. Look at how the face comes forward.

V.Save the light for last

On toned paper the paper itself is your middle tone, so the white is precious. Add highlights at the very end, and only where light actually hits: the wet line of the lower lid, the tip of the nose, the top of the lip. Two or three touches are usually enough.

Try thisFinish a drawing, then allow yourself exactly three white marks. Choose them carefully.
